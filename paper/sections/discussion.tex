\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Calibration.}
The pixel scale rests on the assumption that the limb peak tracks the EFIT outboard LCFS radius with a fixed offset within a camera configuration, but the offset can drift with the emission profile, which depends on density and neutral pressure, and the EFIT boundary carries its own uncertainty of order a centimetre. The bootstrap intervals in Table~\ref{tab:configs} include the scatter that these effects produce but cannot capture a bias common to a whole configuration. The near-uniformity of the filaments in pixel units across configurations whose fitted scales differ by almost a factor of two (Section~\ref{sec:results}) is either a coincidence or a sign that part of the spread in the fitted scales has a non-optical origin, which the archive cannot decide, and no conclusion in the paper therefore depends on comparing physical units between configurations. Every velocity and width in the paper carries a systematic uncertainty of 10--30\% from the scale of its configuration, and a comparison between configurations carries an uncertainty of up to a factor of two. A per-shot Calcam model would remove this uncertainty and is the natural next step if the calibration images can be released, and Appendix~\ref{app:calibration} shows why the within-shot regression cannot substitute for it.

\paragraph{Geometry.}
The strip gives a single row band through the edge whose column axis is close to radial only at the tangency plane, and the measured width is the chord length of an inclined streak, which overestimates the width perpendicular to the filament by $1/\cos\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between the streak and the column axis. This factor is left uncorrected because the angle is poorly determined in a 48-row strip, and since it is roughly constant within a campaign, it shifts $\hat a$ while leaving the velocity--size slope unchanged. Emission is line-integrated along the view, so a bright structure behind the tangency plane projects to a smaller apparent radius, which blurs the SOL distance while leaving the velocity, a time derivative, unaffected.

\paragraph{What the camera sees.}
The $D_\alpha$ emission depends on neutral density as well as plasma density, and because the neutral density falls steeply inside the LCFS, filaments are visible mainly in the SOL and change in brightness as they move. Tracking by centroid is robust to a slowly changing amplitude, whereas optical flow is sensitive to it, which is one reason the flow velocities are low.

\paragraph{Selection.}
The measurement region extends from 10 pixels inboard to 40 pixels outboard of the limb, that is from 3--5~cm inboard to 11--21~cm outboard depending on the configuration, and includes filaments at birth, so velocities in the far SOL may differ. The threshold of 50 tracks per discharge and the exclusion of ELM-adjacent tracks remove some discharges and some of the largest events, so the headline numbers describe inter-ELM and L-mode filaments near the edge.

\paragraph{Model inputs.}
The LCFS temperature is assumed for most discharges and $n_{e,\rm sep}$ is taken as half the line average, and because both enter $a^*$, $v^*$, and $\Lambda$ as power laws with exponents below one, a factor of two in either moves a discharge by less than a factor of two in the regime diagram. The conclusion that the sample straddles $\hat a = 1$ is robust to this uncertainty, whereas $\hat v$ itself is known only to within a factor of two.
